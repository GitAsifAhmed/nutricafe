How much protein does
a student actually need?

Nutri Café TeamFood & nutrition
In this article

    Protein has a reputation problem. Gym culture makes it sound as if you need a shaker of powder after every meal, while many hostel and mess diets are built almost entirely on rice with a thin dal. The truth sits in between, and it is easier to reach than most people think.

    The 0.8 rule

    For healthy adults, the long-standing reference intake is about 0.8 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. The WHO/FAO/UNU expert consultation arrived at a very similar "safe level" of 0.83 g/kg for adults.

    Body weightProtein per day (0.8 g/kg)
    50 kg40 g
    60 kg48 g
    70 kg56 g

    That figure is designed to cover the needs of nearly all healthy adults - it is a floor to reach reliably, not a target to beat. Some people need more: those who train hard most days, people recovering from illness, and pregnant or breastfeeding women. Sports-nutrition bodies commonly suggest around 1.2–2.0 g/kg for athletes in regular training.

    Why rice and dal are a team

    Proteins are made of amino acids, and nine of them are "essential": the body cannot make them, so they must come from food. Eggs, fish, meat and milk contain all nine in useful proportions. Most plant foods are lower in one or two.

    Rice is relatively low in the amino acid lysine. Lentils and other pulses are rich in lysine but lower in methionine, which rice supplies. Eaten across the same day, they fill each other's gaps. The old combination of dal-bhat is good nutrition science, not just habit - its main weakness is that the dal is often too thin and the rice portion too large.

    Dal-bhat is good protein science. The problem is usually a watery dal and a mountain of rice.

    Notice that rice is not protein-free: a plate gives about 4 g. Over three meals, it adds up. A thin, watery home dal has much less protein per bowl than the thick, boiled dal in the table, so the thicker the dal, the better.

    The cheapest protein in Dhaka

    Using our own working ingredient prices, here is roughly what 10 g of protein costs from three staples:

    • Masoor dal (≈ ৳100/kg): about ৳4 for 10 g of protein.
    • Broiler chicken, bone-in (≈ ৳185/kg): about ৳12.
    • Eggs (≈ ৳12 each): about ৳17.

    Dal is by far the cheapest source, which is why a thick bowl of it is one of the most useful things a student budget can buy. Eggs cost more per gram but are complete, quick and portable.

    Spread it out

    Muscle-building studies suggest the body responds best when protein is spread across the day rather than packed into dinner - roughly 0.3–0.4 g/kg per meal, or about 20–30 g for most students. A typical campus day often looks like this instead: a paratha and tea for breakfast (≈ 5 g), a rice-heavy lunch (≈ 12 g), and most of the day's protein at a late dinner.

    Simple fixes: add an egg or chola to breakfast, choose a lunch with a real portion of fish, chicken, egg or thick dal, and keep roasted chola or peanuts for the afternoon.

    Can you eat too much?

    For healthy people, intakes well above 0.8 g/kg are generally considered safe. People with kidney disease are different and should follow their doctor's advice. Protein powders are not necessary for most students - the same protein comes from food, along with fibre, vitamins and minerals that powders do not provide.
