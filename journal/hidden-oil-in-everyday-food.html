The hidden oil in
everyday food

Nutri Café TeamFood & nutrition
In this article

    When people try to eat better, rice usually gets the blame. But in a lot of everyday Bangladeshi food, the ingredient that quietly adds the most energy is not the rice - it is the oil. Oil has no colour on the plate and no taste of its own, so it is easy to forget it is there.

    Every gram of fat carries about 9 kcal, more than twice as much as a gram of carbohydrate or protein (about 4 kcal each). A level tablespoon of soybean or mustard oil weighs roughly 13.8 g, which makes it about 120 kcal - close to the energy in a cup of cooked rice, but with none of the fullness.

    Where the oil goes

    How much oil ends up in your stomach depends on how the food was cooked:

    • Curries, bhuna and bhaji. Almost all of the oil stays in the dish. If the cook pours four tablespoons into a pot that serves four people, each plate carries about a tablespoon - roughly 120 kcal - before you count anything else.
    • Deep-fried snacks. Singara, piyaju, beguni and chop absorb oil while they fry. Battered and porous foods soak up the most, and the longer and cooler the oil, the more they absorb.
    • Shallow-fried food. Some of the oil is left in the pan. This is the one case where not all of the oil you measured is the oil you eat.

    The trouble with reused oil

    Street stalls and busy kitchens often fry in the same oil for hours or days. Every time oil is heated to frying temperature, some of it breaks down: it oxidises, darkens, foams and builds up polar compounds. Repeatedly heated oil can also contain more trans fat than fresh oil.

    Trans fat raises "bad" LDL cholesterol and lowers "good" HDL cholesterol. The World Health Organization estimates that trans fat intake is responsible for up to 500,000 premature deaths from coronary heart disease every year worldwide, and has called for industrially produced trans fat to be eliminated from the food supply.

    Bangladesh has acted on this. In 2021 it adopted a regulation limiting industrially produced trans fat to no more than 2 g per 100 g of total fat in oils, fats and foods. That protects the oil you buy - but it cannot control what happens to that oil after the tenth batch of piyaju.

    Mustard, soybean or rice bran?

    All cooking oils carry nearly the same energy - about 900 kcal per 100 g in the Food Composition Table for Bangladesh - so switching oil does not cut calories. The difference is in the type of fat:

    • Mustard oil is rich in monounsaturated fat and contains some plant omega-3 (alpha-linolenic acid). It gives Bengali dishes their sharp, familiar flavour.
    • Soybean oil is mostly polyunsaturated fat and is the most common oil in Dhaka kitchens.
    • Rice bran oil has a high smoke point, which makes it useful for frying.
    • Palm oil and vanaspati (dalda) are higher in saturated fat. WHO advises keeping saturated fat below 10% of daily energy and replacing it with unsaturated fats where possible.

    The most useful choice is not which oil, but how much.

    Five ways to use less without losing flavour

    1. Measure, don't pour. Keep a tablespoon next to the stove. Most people pour two or three times what they think.
    2. Bhuna with water. Start the masala with a little oil, then add splashes of water as it catches. The spices still cook down and darken.
    3. Use a heavy or non-stick pan. Food sticks less, so you need less oil to stop it burning.
    4. Cook first, temper last. Boil or steam dal and vegetables, then finish them with a phoron of whole spices fried in a single teaspoon of oil. The aroma comes from the tempering, not the quantity.
    5. Retire tired oil. If frying oil has darkened, foams, smokes early or smells sharp, throw it away rather than topping it up.
